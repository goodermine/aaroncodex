# Vocal Onset: How Notes Begin

*Phonation and pitch attack in contemporary singing — an evidence-graded reference.*

> **Evidence grades used throughout.** **Strong** = replicated peer-reviewed or
> mechanistic. **Moderate** = single studies, small samples, or reasoned
> extrapolation. **Tradition** = coach/pedagogy account, physiologically
> plausible but not directly validated for the stated outcome. Read the "Where
> the science is strong vs. where it is tradition" section before treating any
> specific drill as a proven prescription.

## TL;DR

**"Onset" names two separable things.** *Phonation onset* (vocal attack) is the
laryngeal timing of vocal-fold adduction against airflow — breathy, balanced or
pressed. *Pitch onset* (note approach) is whether the fundamental (F0) arrives on
target, scoops up from below, or overshoots from above. A note can be balanced in
attack yet scooped in pitch, and vice versa; the two are governed by different
mechanisms and need different drills.

The science is strongest on phonation-onset physiology and on the risks of
habitual hard onset — hard glottal attack was present in 65% of hyperfunctional
voice patients (Morrison, Nichol & Rammage 1986) and at higher frequency across
all disordered groups than in healthy controls (Andrade et al. 2000). Pitch-onset
behaviour is measurable (an onset transient of ~120 ms; Dai & Dixon 2019, JASA)
but remediation evidence is largely inferred from motor-learning and
auditory-feedback studies rather than tested directly on scooping.

**For a singer whose centring is already professional (~20–25 cents) but who
scoops into roughly half of entries from nearly a semitone below, overshoots
about a third, and lands only about a quarter clean (against a professional
median near a third clean), this is a pitch-onset motor-targeting problem — not
an attack-type problem and not an intonation problem.** Priority: pre-hearing
(audiation) plus staccato "land-it" drills for scoops, and slow-approach /
"aim-short" drills for overshoots.

## Key findings

1. **Two distinct phenomena share one word.** Phonation onset is a laryngeal
   coordination event (adduction vs. airflow timing); pitch onset is a
   pitch-targeting/motor event (F0 trajectory in the first ~100–250 ms). Most
   pedagogy and voice science under "onset" addresses phonation onset. The
   pitch-onset sense is studied mainly in music-psychology and
   music-information-retrieval literature under "intonation trajectory," "scoop,"
   and "pitch imitation." **[Strong]**
2. **Three canonical phonation-onset types are well established:**
   breathy/aspirate (airflow precedes adduction), balanced/simultaneous
   (adduction and airflow coincide at phonation threshold) and
   pressed/hard/glottal (full adduction precedes airflow). These map onto
   measurable vocal-attack-time (VAT) values: positive for breathy, near-zero for
   balanced, negative for hard (Orlikoff, Deliyski & Baken 2009). **[Strong]**
3. **Balanced onset is the healthy default; the others are legitimate stylistic
   tools.** Breathy onset conveys intimacy and is largely microphone-dependent;
   hard onset gives emphasis and effects. Habitual hard onset is a phonotrauma
   risk marker. **[Strong physiology; Moderate stylistic use]**
4. **Habitual pressed/glottal onset is associated with vocal pathology.** Hard
   glottal attack was found in 65% of hyperfunctional voice patients (Morrison,
   Nichol & Rammage 1986) and at higher frequency in all disordered groups vs.
   controls (Andrade et al. 2000, 147 subjects). It reflects high vocal-fold
   collision forces implicated in nodule/polyp formation. **[Strong]**
5. **Pitch onset is a real, measurable trajectory.** Skilled singers show a short
   onset transient (~120 ms; Dai & Dixon 2019, JASA 146:1005–1014) with a "clear
   direction"; less-trained singers show uneven, unstable, inconsistent onsets.
   Even highly trained singers commonly begin notes with some pitch fluctuation.
   **[Strong]**
6. **Scooping and overshooting are different failures with different causes.**
   Scooping (arriving flat and sliding up) is characteristic of feedback-driven
   *searching* — even highly trained singers "often do not start a sung pitch on
   the precise target" (Larrouy-Maestri & Pfordresher 2018, citing Hutchins &
   Campbell 2009; Mori et al. 2004). Overshooting (arriving sharp and settling) is
   more common after leaps and reflects feedforward *mis-scaling*: singers
   "overshoot the target pitch and then adjust downward after singing a lower
   pitch, while after a high pitch they reach the target almost immediately" (Dai
   & Dixon 2019). **[Strong phenomenon; Moderate causal split]**
7. **Expertise shifts pitch control from feedback to feedforward.** Trained
   singers rely less on real-time auditory feedback and more on predictive
   internal models; they compensate for feedback perturbations faster, smaller
   and later, and retain adaptations longer (Zarate et al.; Jones & Keough 2008).
   This predicts that durable onset accuracy comes from better *pre-planning*
   (audiation), not more mid-note correction. **[Strong]**
8. **Auditory imagery predicts pitch accuracy.** Poor-pitch singing is largely a
   sensorimotor-translation deficit, not a perceptual one (Pfordresher & Brown
   2007), and auditory-imagery ability correlates with singing accuracy and
   robustness to feedback disruption (Pfordresher & Halpern 2013; Reed, Pearce &
   McPherson 2024). This is the empirical basis for "pre-hear the note" drills.
   **[Strong correlation; Moderate training transfer]**
9. **Onset re-training follows general motor-learning principles.** Blocked,
   high-feedback practice speeds early acquisition, but variable/random,
   low-feedback practice yields better retention and transfer — shown directly in
   a voice-motor task (learning "twang," *Journal of Voice* 2023) and in the
   speech-motor-learning literature (Maas et al. 2008). **[Strong]**
10. **Measurement is reliable for attack type and onset trajectory on clean solo
    audio, but breaks on separated stems.** VAT needs synchronous EGG + acoustic
    recording; cents-deviation onset analysis needs a good pitch tracker (CREPE
    maintains >90% raw pitch accuracy even at a strict 10-cent threshold; Kim et
    al. 2018). Both degrade badly on stem-separated vocals, where bleed and
    artefacts corrupt exactly the low-energy note-start region. **[Strong]**

## Details

### 1. The central distinction: phonation onset vs. pitch onset

**Phonation onset** (vocal attack) is the laryngeal event at the start of a note
— the relative timing of vocal-fold adduction and the establishment of subglottal
driving pressure. **Pitch onset** (note approach) is the frequency behaviour of
that same note-start — whether F0 lands on, below or above the target.

**They are dissociable.** You can have a clean balanced attack that is scooped in
pitch (folds meet neatly at threshold, but F0 rises into the target over ~150 ms).
You can have a pressed attack that is dead-on in pitch (hard glottal closure, F0
correct from the first cycle). A breathy attack can be perfectly centred or badly
scooped. The failures belong to different categories:

- **Phonation-onset failures:** chronic breathiness (hypoadduction), chronic hard
  attack (hyperadduction), audible click/pop, delayed voicing.
- **Pitch-onset failures:** scooping (flat approach from below), overshooting
  (sharp approach from above), unstable "searching" wobble at the start.

**They interact at two points.** First, a hard glottal onset tends to fix F0
quickly (folds already fully adducted → less searching), which is why some singers
unconsciously *press* to hit tricky entries — trading a pitch-onset problem for a
phonation-health problem. Second, register negotiation affects both: a difficult
passaggio entry can produce both a breathy/unstable attack and a scoop as the
singer "finds" the coordination.

### 2. Phonation onset: physiology and acoustics

Voice onset requires synchronising glottal adduction with a subglottal
overpressure above phonation threshold pressure (PTP) — the minimum subglottal
pressure to initiate self-sustaining oscillation (Titze; National Center for Voice
and Speech). PTP falls with reduced vocal-fold stiffness, thinner medial surface,
and adduction near (not past) the optimum. In an in-vivo canine model, combined
lateral cricoarytenoid + thyroarytenoid activation gave stable, low-noise onset,
whereas single-muscle activation gave aperiodic or noisy starts.

The three types differ in the adduction–airflow interval:

- **Aspirate/breathy:** folds still abducted as airflow starts; audible /h/-like
  turbulence precedes voicing; the fundamental dominates while higher overtones
  arrive slightly delayed.
- **Balanced/simultaneous:** adduction and airflow coincide at threshold;
  overtones arrive together; cleanest energy build-up.
- **Hard/glottal:** folds fully adducted before airflow; pressure builds behind a
  closed glottis and releases abruptly ("uh-oh"); higher partials arrive
  simultaneously with an abrupt onset.

**EGG and acoustic markers.** Electroglottography (EGG) tracks vocal-fold contact
area; the contact quotient (CQ_EGG) estimates the fraction of the cycle in contact
and rises with adduction and SPL (Orlikoff 1991: mean CQ ≈ 0.57). **Caution:**
CQ_EGG is not interchangeable with the aerodynamic closed quotient — different
algorithms give offsets up to ~0.2–0.3 of a cycle, and the two "relate to
different physiological phenomena" (Herbst & Ternström). EGG cannot be computed
for breathy phonation without complete closure. The most clinically portable onset
marker is **vocal attack time (VAT)** — the cross-correlation lag between the rise
of the acoustic and EGG signals: positive for breathy, ≈0 for balanced, negative
for hard (Orlikoff, Deliyski & Baken 2009, validated against high-speed
videoendoscopy; normative data in Roark, Watson & Baken 2012).

### 3. Stylistic and health trade-offs of each type

| Onset type | Physiology | Markers | Good for | Bad for / risks | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Breathy / aspirate** | Airflow before adduction; incomplete closure | Positive VAT; pre-voicing noise; low CQ | Intimacy, soft pop/R&B/jazz entries; mic-dependent | Doesn't project unamplified; wastes air; chronic use may signal hypoadduction | Strong physiology; Moderate stylistic |
| **Balanced / simultaneous** | Adduction meets airflow at threshold | VAT ≈ 0; clean energy rise | Healthy default across styles | Little downside; less "characterful" | Strong |
| **Pressed / hard / glottal** | Full adduction before airflow; pressure release | Negative VAT; abrupt rise; high collision force | Emphasis, accents, effects (grunts, cries) | Phonotrauma risk if habitual (nodules, polyps, MTD) | Strong risk; Moderate occasional use |

CCM singers deliberately exploit a wider onset palette than classical singers —
aspirate, fry-onset, cry, growl and glottal attacks are style-defining tools
(McCoy). In bossa nova and much soft pop the default phonation setting is breathy.
None of this contradicts the health finding: **stylistic, chosen, occasional hard
onset differs fundamentally from habitual, involuntary pressing.**

### 4. Health: stop-rules and red flags

**Habitual hard glottal attack is a phonotrauma risk.** Retrospective clinical
data associate it with muscle-tension dysphonia (MTD) and benign fold lesions: 65%
of hyperfunctional patients presented with hard glottal attack (Morrison, Nichol &
Rammage 1986), and Andrade et al. (2000) found higher frequencies in all
voice-disordered groups than in controls. The mechanism is elevated collision
stress at the mid-membranous fold.

**Stop and rest** if onset work produces pain, a sensation of effortful "hitting,"
or persistent throat tightening. Pain is never a training target.

**See a laryngologist/SLP** if any of the following persist beyond ~2 weeks
despite rest: hoarseness that won't clear; loss of the top of the range; a note
that "won't start" (new delayed/breathy onset); pitch breaks at onset; vocal
fatigue with pain; or a habitual hard attack you cannot release voluntarily. New
persistent breathy onset can signal hypoadduction/paresis; new persistent hard
onset can signal compensatory hyperfunction. Either warrants stroboscopy. These
are screening heuristics, not diagnosis.

### 5. Pitch onset: why singers scoop and overshoot

Pitch production is governed by interacting **feedforward** (predictive,
internal-model) and **feedback** (auditory-error-correcting) systems. The
pitch-shift reflex — an involuntary, ~100 ms-latency compensatory F0 change
opposing an imposed feedback shift (Burnett et al. 1998; Hain et al. 2000) —
demonstrates the feedback loop directly. Crucially, experts weight feedforward
more heavily: trained singers compensate less on initial exposure to perturbation
but retain adaptations longer, indicating reliance on stable internal models
rather than moment-to-moment correction (Jones & Keough 2008).

This framework explains the two error types:

- **Scooping is a feedback-driven search:** the singer starts flat and rides F0 up
  until auditory feedback confirms the target. Worst when the target is uncertain
  (weak pre-hearing), on high notes, and across register transitions.
- **Overshooting is a feedforward scaling error:** the motor command over-scales
  the interval (especially after ascending leaps), then settles.

So the two have **different causes and different fixes:** scooping is
under-specifying the target and needs better target pre-specification plus a
committed, non-searching attack; overshooting is over-scaling and needs interval
calibration plus a decelerated, "aim-short" approach.

Poor-pitch singing generally is a sensorimotor-translation deficit, not a
perceptual one — poor-pitch singers usually discriminate pitch normally
(Pfordresher & Brown 2007; Hutchins & Peretz 2012). **For a professional-level
singer whose centring is already accurate, the deficit is even more clearly at the
targeting/onset stage, not perception or steady-state control.**

### 6. What the pitch-onset numbers actually show

| Failure | Direction | Magnitude / timing (illustrative) | Likely cause | Primary fix |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Scoop** | Starts flat, rises to target | Occasional singers: starting scoops M ≈ 63 cents (Larrouy-Maestri & Pfordresher 2018); onset transient ~120 ms (Dai & Dixon 2019) | Feedback search; weak audiation; uncertain/high/register-crossing target | Audiation + staccato "land-it"; interval pre-tuning |
| **Overshoot** | Starts sharp, settles down | Common after ascending leaps; convex F0 shape | Feedforward over-scaling of the interval | Slow decelerating approach; "aim-short"; isolate the leap |
| **Search / wobble** | Unstable, no clear direction | Larger, uneven in untrained singers | Poor internal model of target | Pre-hear + commit; slow blocked repetition |

**Supporting figures.**

- **Onset transients are ~120 ms.** In >35,000 notes of unaccompanied SATB quartet
  singing, Dai & Dixon (2019) found transients of ~120 ms at both note ends — about
  15–20% of a mean 0.69 s note. Trained singers' transients had "a clear
  direction"; less-trained were "uneven."
- **Direction depends on the previous note:** overshoot-then-settle after a lower
  prior pitch; near-immediate landing after a higher prior pitch. Male singers
  tended to start high and adjust down; female singers showed a convex shape —
  starting low, overshooting, then settling.
- **Scoop magnitudes are large and mostly involuntary in occasional singers.**
  Larrouy-Maestri & Pfordresher (2018), 1,461 sung tones from 29 occasional
  singers: starting scoops M = 63.3 cents (SD 22.8), ending scoops M = 99.2 (SD
  46.6); downward scoops (M = 89.7) exceeded upward (M = 72.8); range 58–159 cents.
  Singers varied "primarily in the magnitude of scoops but not in the duration."
- **Scoops affect how in-tune a note sounds.** Listeners "do not simply disregard
  these scoops"; scooped tones were rated less in-tune than clean ones, and ending
  scoops were penalised more than starting scoops.
- **The classic "start flat, correct" claim.** Seashore (1938) wrote that "long
  notes tend to begin slightly flat and are gradually corrected, as if by
  hearing." *Caveat:* the widely repeated "about 9 cents flat for the first 200
  ms" is a secondary gloss, not Seashore's own figure (one source even reports ~90
  cents); treat only the ~200 ms window and the flat→corrected direction as
  reliable.
- **Professional intonation spread.** Trained singers typically centre within
  ~20–30 cents under normal feedback (Watts, Murphy & Barnes-Burroughs 2003);
  professionals average ~0.3 semitone deviation vs. ~0.6 for accurate amateurs and
  >1 semitone for poor-pitch singers.

### 7. Measurement — where it is reliable and where it breaks

**Phonation-onset (attack-type) measurement.** Best: synchronous EGG + microphone
→ VAT (objective, validated). Cheaper: acoustic rise time and spectral onset slope.
CQ_EGG characterises steady-state adduction but is algorithm-dependent, is not
equal to the aerodynamic closed quotient, and is unusable for breathy phonation.

**Pitch-onset measurement.** Extract F0 with a modern tracker (CREPE or pYIN;
~90–91% raw pitch accuracy on clean monophonic vocals, and CREPE maintains >90%
even at a strict 10-cent tolerance — Kim et al. 2018). Then quantify: cents
deviation over the first 100–250 ms, scoop depth (cents below target at note start
vs. the asymptote), and overshoot (peak cents above target). Note-onset boundaries
themselves are error-prone — trackers misplace boundaries and produce octave
errors precisely at low-energy note starts (Mauch & Dixon).

**Where consumer/automated measurement breaks:**

- **Stem separation.** Analysing a vocal isolated from a mix introduces bleed and
  artefacts. Voicing detection and F0 estimation degrade as SNR falls; even good
  separators drop well below ~80% success at low SNR, and heavy vocal effects
  (auto-tune, distortion) confuse formant-trained models. **The damage
  concentrates in the quiet onset region — exactly where scoop/overshoot lives —
  so automated scoop statistics from stem-separated audio can be artefacts, not
  singing.**
- **Pitch-tracker onset errors.** Trackers stabilise a few frames in, so the first
  ~20–60 ms may be unreliable; octave halving/doubling is common at onsets.
- **Auto-tuned or comped commercial tracks** already have onsets edited; measuring
  "scoop rate" off a released master measures the producer, not the singer.

*Reliable practice:* record a clean solo dry vocal (lossless WAV/FLAC), verify
tracker output against the spectrogram, and treat the first ~2–3 frames as
suspect.

### 8. Motor learning and transfer for onset re-training

Both attack-type and pitch-onset changes are motor-skill re-learning problems and
obey the same principles (Maas et al. 2008; Schmidt & Lee):

- **Blocked → random/variable.** Start blocked (same note/interval repeated) to
  acquire the coordination fast, then move to random/variable (mixed intervals,
  keys, vowels, tempi) for retention and transfer. In a direct voice-motor study,
  random practice reduced immediate performance but improved long-term learning.
- **High → low, delayed, self-evaluated feedback.** Frequent immediate feedback (a
  tuner) helps acquisition but degrades retention if maintained; fade to
  intermittent, summary, delayed feedback and prompt self-judgement ("was that
  clean?") before revealing the tuner.
- **External focus.** Cue the sound/target ("ping the centre of the note") rather
  than the body ("close your folds"); external attentional focus improves motor
  learning.
- **Transfer is the hard part.** Skills learned in isolated drills notoriously
  fail to carry into performance. Bridge deliberately: drill → same onset in a
  sung phrase → in a full song → in performance-like conditions (standing, moving,
  with lyrics — recall that lyric/phonetic variability worsens pitch tracking and
  accuracy).

## Where the science is strong vs. where it is tradition

**Strong science (peer-reviewed, replicated or mechanistic):** the three-way
physiological taxonomy of phonation onset and its adduction–airflow-timing basis;
VAT as a validated attack-type marker; PTP as the onset pressure threshold; hard
glottal attack's association with vocal hyperfunction and benign lesions; the
feedforward/feedback duality of pitch control and the pitch-shift reflex; poor-pitch
singing as sensorimotor-translation rather than perceptual; onset transients ~120
ms and scoop magnitudes and their perceptual salience; motor-learning
practice/feedback structure effects.

**Effective tradition / heuristic (plausible, widely taught, not directly
validated for this outcome):**

- **Specific onset drills** — staccato "hey/uh-oh/hah," silent-h aspirate onset,
  hum-to-slide — are standard studio practice with sound physiological rationale
  but little controlled evidence they re-train an onset better than alternatives.
  SOVT/straw work is better-supported (Titze 2006; Verdolini et al. 1998) for
  reducing *pressed* phonation, but that evidence concerns resonance/effort and
  nodule reduction, not pitch-onset accuracy. **[Tradition → Moderate]**
- **Audiation / "pre-hear the note"** is strongly motivated by imagery–accuracy
  correlations, but direct evidence that training it reduces scooping specifically
  is thin. **[Moderate]**
- **Interval "pre-tuning," "aim-short" for overshoot, and target-and-approach
  drills** are reasoned extrapolations from feedforward/feedback theory, not tested
  protocols. **[Tradition/extrapolation]**
- **Vowel heuristics** ("oo/oh" relaxes an over-glottal onset; "ah/ee" firms a
  breathy one) are coach accounts. **[Tradition]**

## Recommendations

For a singer whose centring is professional (~20–25 cents) but who scoops ~50% of
entries from nearly a semitone below, overshoots ~33%, and lands ~25% clean vs. a
professional median near ~33%. *All specific rep/pitch/timing figures below are
illustrative, not universal prescriptions.* **Interpretation: a pitch-onset
motor-targeting problem** — not attack type (unless a laryngeal cause is found) and
not intonation (steady-state centring is already good). Goal: raise clean-onset
rate from ~25% toward ≥33% and beyond.

**Stage 0 — Diagnose and separate the two problems (1 session).** Record a dry,
clean solo vocal (lossless); extract F0 (CREPE/pYIN); verify against the
spectrogram. *Do not analyse a stem separated from a mix.* Tag each entry: scoop
(>~30 cents flat, rising), overshoot (starts sharp, settling) or clean. Confirm the
split by previous-note direction. Separately, check attack type by ear or VAT — if
entries are also pressed or breathy, treat that in parallel, but do not assume it.

**Stage 1 — Target pre-specification (audiation) — attacks the scoop root cause.**
Before each entry, silently pre-hear the exact pitch for one beat, then start.
Blocked practice on the specific problem entries drawn from real repertoire.
Advance when scoop rate on drilled notes drops noticeably and you can feel
commitment to the pitch before sound. **[Moderate]**

**Stage 2 — Split drills by error type (run concurrently, blocked first).**

- **For scoops:** staccato "land-it" onsets — short, balanced, dead-on repeated
  attacks on the target; then two-note "pre-tune the interval" (hear the lower
  note, silently hear the target, attack the target directly with no glide). Cue:
  **"arrive, don't reach."**
- **For overshoots:** slow, decelerating legato approach and "aim-short" leaps —
  deliberately target a hair under the note after big ascending intervals, then let
  it settle; practise the leap interval in isolation to recalibrate feedforward
  scaling. Cue: **"place it, don't throw it."**
- **Keep attacks balanced throughout.** If you catch yourself pressing to hit an
  entry, you are trading a pitch problem for a health risk — stop and re-set with
  an SOVT (straw/lip-trill) onset. **[Tradition/Moderate]**

**Stage 3 — Fade feedback, add variability, transfer.** Move from tuner-on-every-note
to intermittent/delayed feedback; self-rate each entry before checking. Randomise
keys, vowels, intervals, tempi; then re-embed the corrected onsets into full
phrases → whole songs → performance conditions. **[Strong]**

**Metrics and thresholds that would change the plan:**

- Re-measure clean-onset rate every 2–3 weeks on *fresh* repertoire (not the
  drilled items). Target trajectory: 25% → ~33% → higher.
- If clean rate rises in drills but not in songs, the bottleneck is **transfer** —
  increase variable practice and performance simulation; reduce blocked drilling.
- If scoops persist despite strong audiation, suspect a **register/passaggio**
  issue at those specific pitches — address registration there.
- **Escalate to a laryngologist/SLP** if onsets come with pain, hoarseness, new
  breathiness, or an inability to start certain notes — a motor-targeting plan is
  inappropriate until an organic cause is excluded.

## Caveats

- **Terminology is contested.** "Onset," "attack" and "initiation" are used
  inconsistently; "balanced/simultaneous/coordinated/clean" are near-synonyms, as
  are "hard/pressed/glottal/hyperfunctional." "Closed quotient" (aerodynamic) and
  "contact quotient" (EGG) are routinely conflated but refer to different
  phenomena. This document uses **phonation onset** and **pitch onset** to keep the
  two senses apart, and **breathy / balanced / pressed** and **clean / scoop /
  overshoot** as the standard labels.
- **Much pitch-onset evidence comes from occasional/amateur or choral singers**, or
  from imitation tasks with synthetic stimuli — not professional soloists singing
  repertoire. The scoop-magnitude figures are from occasional singers; the 120 ms
  transient is from amateur choral quartets. Extrapolate to a professional pop
  soloist with caution.
- **Several classic figures are unverified at primary-source level.** Seashore's "9
  cents / 200 ms" is a secondary gloss; Prame's "+42 to −44 cents" spread is cited
  from secondary sources only.
- **Drill efficacy is mostly tradition.** The specific onset and
  anti-scoop/anti-overshoot drills rest on physiological rationale, motor-learning
  theory and coach accounts — not on controlled trials showing they beat
  alternatives for onset accuracy. Reasonable and low-risk, but Moderate-to-Tradition.
- **Measurement fragility is real.** Automated onset/scoop statistics are only as
  good as the pitch tracker and the audio; stem-separated, auto-tuned or lossy
  audio can manufacture apparent scoops. Trust only clean, dry, verified recordings.
- **Small studies.** Several cited results (in-vivo animal PTP work, VAT normative
  samples, voice-motor-learning studies) come from small samples; treat effect
  sizes as indicative.
