# Formants — Deep & Wide

**The acoustics of the singing voice, from the ground up**

*Companion to The Four-Machine Course — the theory behind Machine 3 (the filter).*

Once this clicks it reorganises how you hear every voice you will ever listen to. The one sentence to carry the whole document: **the cords make the note, but the tube decides whether it rings, carries, and stays alive — and the tube is yours to shape, note by note.**

---

## 1. The foundation, one level deeper

> Source and filter are independent — two sets of hands on two different dials.

The cords make a raw buzz (the source); the vocal tract sculpts it (the filter). The crucial word is *independent*: you can hold the pitch dead still and completely transform the sound by reshaping the tube, and you can hold the vowel still and run the pitch anywhere. Two control systems, two dials.

The buzz is not one clean frequency. It is a **fundamental** (the pitch) plus a ladder of **harmonics** stacked above it at exact whole-number multiples: sing 220 Hz and you also produce 440, 660, 880, 1100, 1320... each quieter than the last, marching up forever in equal steps. Your brain fuses them into one tone with a colour. That ladder is the raw material — and the filter's whole job is to decide which rungs get amplified and which get killed.

---

## 2. What a formant physically is

> A region of amplification the tube provides — not a sound the cords make.

Your vocal tract is a tube of air — roughly 17 cm long, closed at one end (the cords), open at the other (the lips). Every tube of air has natural frequencies at which it resonates and amplifies sound. Blow across a bottle: the note you get is that bottle's resonant frequency. Your vocal tract is a bottle you reshape in real time with tongue, jaw and lips. Those resonant frequencies are the **formants**.

A formant is a *band* of frequency the tube boosts. Any harmonic from the source that lands inside a formant band rings out loud; any harmonic in a dead zone between formants stays quiet. The formant is not something the cords produce — it is amplification supplied by the shape of the tube.

### Why the formants sit where they do

A tube closed at one end and open at the other is a **quarter-wave resonator**: its lowest resonance has a wavelength four times the tube length. For a 17.5 cm tract with the speed of sound about 343 m/s, that first resonance is 343 / (4 x 0.175) ~ **490 Hz**. A uniform tube resonates at odd multiples of that, giving the neutral-vowel series of roughly **500, 1500, 2500, 3500 Hz** — the "uh" (schwa) fingerprint, falling straight out of the tube length.

Every vowel is a distortion of that neutral series, made by narrowing the tube in one place and widening it in another. Where you constrict determines which formant moves.

## 3. The formants, one by one

> Only the lowest two decide the vowel. The higher ones carry timbre and ring.

*(Rough adult-male ranges.)*

| Formant | Governed mainly by | Typical range | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **F1** | Jaw / mouth openness | ~250-850 Hz | Openness of the vowel. Open jaw (*ah*) -> high F1; closed (*ee*, *oo*) -> low F1 |
| **F2** | Tongue front / back | ~600-2400 Hz | Frontness. Tongue forward (*ee*) -> high F2; tongue back (*oo*) -> low F2 |
| **F3** | Tongue tip; space behind teeth | ~2400-3000 Hz | Fine timbre; drops for "r"; part of the ring cluster |
| **F4 / F5** | Overall tract length; larynx tube | ~3000-4500 Hz | Voice "signature"; when clustered, the professional ring |

**The rule of thumb that makes them usable:** F1 tracks how open your mouth is (open wider -> F1 rises), and F2 tracks tongue front-to-back. "Ee" (tongue high, forward) has a sky-high F2; "oo" (tongue back, lips round) has a low F2 — that single difference is the whole distance between those two words on the same pitch.

## 4. The vowel space — every vowel is a coordinate

> A vowel is not a mouth-shape you memorised — it is a formant pattern your ear recognises.

Plot F1 on one axis and F2 on the other and every vowel becomes a point — an (F1, F2) coordinate. Linguists call it the **vowel quadrilateral**. Its corners:

- **"ee"** — low F1, high F2 *(top-left)*
- **"oo"** — low F1, low F2 *(top-right)*
- **"ah"** — high F1, mid F2 *(bottom)*

Everything else — *eh, ih, aw, uh* — fills the space between.

### Prove it to yourself

- Sing "ee - eh - ah - oh - oo" on one held pitch. The cords never change.
- The only thing moving is the tube shape — therefore the formants — yet your ear hears five different sounds.
- That difference *is* the formants relocating. There is nothing else it could be: the source is constant.

This map is essentially the same for every human on earth — the only reason we can understand each other's vowels at all. Your "ah" and mine land in the same neighbourhood even if our voices sound nothing alike, because the formant pattern carries the vowel, independent of pitch or timbre.

## 5. Harmonics meet formants — the actual interaction

> Positioning your formants so that strong harmonics land on them — that is resonant singing.

The source hands up a ladder of harmonics; the filter has a few resonant peaks. Wherever a harmonic lands on a formant, it is amplified and the note gains power and ring. Wherever harmonics fall in the gaps, that energy is lost.

At low pitches this is automatic — the harmonics are packed close (220 Hz apart at A3), so there is always one near every formant and the tube can't miss. But as you climb, the harmonics spread apart (440 Hz apart at A4; about 700 Hz at F5). Now there are wide gaps between rungs, and a formant can sit in a gap catching nothing.

The note goes weak and hollow — **not because the cords failed, but because the tube's amplification missed the harmonics.** That is an acoustic failure, and it has an acoustic fix.

---

## 6. Formant tuning I — the F1 ceiling

> The single most useful acoustic fact for a singer. Why some high notes wall out.

Every vowel has an F1 value, and **your fundamental cannot climb above F1 while keeping that vowel.** Once your pitch reaches the vowel's F1 you hit a wall: the fundamental is now higher than the formant meant to support it, resonance drops out, and the note turns thin, strained, or slams into a ceiling.

The consequence explains something you have felt for years:

- **Closed vowels have low F1** — "ee" (~300 Hz), "oo" (~350 Hz). You hit their ceiling early, low in your range. This is exactly why belting "ee" or "oo" up high feels impossible.
- **Open vowels have high F1** — "ah" (~700-800 Hz). You can climb much further before trouble. This is why belting "ah" high feels natural, and why almost every big high note in rock and soul lands on an open, wide vowel.

Same note, different vowel, completely different difficulty — and now you know precisely why. It is not the note. It is whether your pitch has overshot that vowel's F1.

## 7. Formant tuning II — the strategies

> If the tube is fighting you, retune it as you ascend. This is vowel modification / formant tuning.

| Strategy | The move | What it does acoustically | Who uses it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **F1 tracking** | Open the jaw more as you go up | Raises F1 to stay above the rising pitch, so the vowel doesn't collapse | Everyone on high notes; extreme in sopranos |
| **Tune F1 to H2** | Bright, forward, wide shape so F1 sits on the 2nd harmonic (2x pitch) | Locks a strong harmonic onto F1 -> the belt stays loud and bright | Belt: rock, soul, gospel, theatre |
| **Vowel migration** | Let "ee"->"ih"->"eh", or "oo"->"uh" as you climb | Nudges the formant into a spot where a harmonic catches | All high singing |
| **Covering** | Slightly round and lower the larynx through the passaggio | Smooths the gear-change, keeps tone even, avoids a shouty break | Classical / legit technique |

**The visible tell of F1 tracking:** watch any singer's jaw drop open on their highest notes. Opera, belt, gospel — the jaw falls open not for drama but because opening it raises F1 to chase the pitch upward and keep the note resonant. Once you know it, you can't unsee it.

> **For your voice** — a rock singer with a fragile top — the highest-leverage combination is **F1 tracking + vowel migration**: as you approach and pass your transition, open the shape and let closed vowels drift more open. A word that walls out on a closed vowel will often free up instantly if you migrate it toward "eh" or "ah" and drop the jaw. Try it on the exact word that's failing.

## 8. The singer's formant — the professional "ring"

> How an opera singer is audible over an 80-piece orchestra with no microphone.

Trained voices develop a cluster of the upper formants (F3, F4, F5) bunched around **2.8-3.4 kHz**, producing a strong concentrated peak — the **singer's formant**. Two things make it devastating:

1. It sits in a band where orchestral instruments have little energy, so the voice occupies a clear channel above the wall of sound.
2. Human hearing is most sensitive right around 3 kHz (the ear canal itself resonates there), so a peak in that band punches far above its actual loudness.

Physically it is made by a **lowered larynx forming a wide pharynx above a narrow epilaryngeal tube** — the collar of space just above the cords. When that collar is about a sixth the width of the pharynx it acts as its own ~3 kHz resonator and clusters the top formants together (Sundberg).

This is why classical training obsesses over the low, settled larynx and the "open throat." You sing into a mic so you don't strictly need it — but the sensation of "ring" or "ping" high in the face is you brushing against this cluster.

---

## 9. Twang — the rock and belt shortcut to ring

> The practical goldmine, and widely misunderstood.

You can get a bright, loud, carrying ping around **2.5-3.5 kHz** without lowering the larynx at all — instead you narrow the epilaryngeal tube with the **aryepiglottic sphincter**, the little valve just above the cords. This is **twang**. It produces a concentrated high-frequency resonance that reads to the ear as loud and cutting while costing almost no extra air or effort. It is the "cry," the "ping," the edge in country, rock, gospel, soul and theatre belt.

**Twang is not nasality.** It is made in the epilarynx and works with the soft palate fully closed and no air through the nose. It sounds bright and forward, which people mislabel as "nasal," but the mechanism is different.

**Twang is efficiency, not force.** It makes you louder by resonance, not by pushing harder — exactly what a rock singer wants for stamina. It is the antidote to shouting: the same carrying power for a fraction of the effort. If you want your top to cut through a band without leaning on it, narrow that collar, find the bright "witchy / duck-call" edge, and blend it back to taste.

## 10. The other tuning levers — larynx height and lips

> Two dials that shift the whole formant set at once.

**Larynx height changes the tube length**, so it moves every formant together.

- Lower the larynx -> longer tube -> all formants shift **down** -> darker, rounder, warmer, "covered," operatic.
- Raise it -> shorter tube -> formants shift **up** -> brighter, younger, "yell-ier," more pop/rock.

Belters often ride a slightly higher larynx (brightness) plus twang (ring); classical rides a lower larynx (warmth) plus the singer's formant. Neither is "correct" — they are stylistic destinations, and you choose.

**Lip shape changes the effective tube length.** Rounding and protruding the lips lengthens the tube -> lowers the formants (darker, hootier). Spreading the lips (a slight smile) shortens it -> raises the formants (brighter, more forward). A fast fine-tuning tool: round to darken and warm a note, spread to brighten and add cut. Cheap, immediate, entirely under conscious control.

---

## 11. Nasality and anti-formants

> The side-branch that subtracts as well as adds.

Lower the soft palate and you couple the nasal cavity into the tract, adding a side-branch. That does two things: it introduces nasal formants, and — more importantly — it creates **anti-formants** (or zeros): frequencies where energy is actively *cancelled* rather than boosted.

So nasality doesn't just add resonance, it subtracts it in places, which is why heavy nasality can sound thin or pinched. A touch can be used deliberately as a brightness or placement cue — but keep it separate in your mind from **twang** (section 9). They are different tools, and twang is usually the one you actually want for carrying power.

## 12. How to actually hear formants

> Theory becomes real the moment you hear a formant move on its own.

### The whisper trick — formants naked, source switched off

- Whisper "ee - eh - ah - oh - oo" slowly. Whispering has no fundamental — the cords make no pitch.
- So what you hear is purely the tube resonances — the formants themselves — as bands of noise.
- You'll clearly hear the resonance swoop down and around as the vowel changes. That swoop is F1 and F2 moving.

### Overtone singing — the killer demonstration

- Sing a steady low drone. Very slowly move tongue and lips as if going "ee" -> "oo" — but keep the pitch dead still.
- At certain shapes a high whistling tone pops out above the drone, and you can move it like a melody.
- You are sweeping a formant across your harmonic ladder, amplifying single harmonics until they whistle out.
- It is the purest proof of the whole theory: the formant is selecting harmonics out of the source.

Ten minutes of this and formants stop being abstract forever.

## 13. What this means for your voice

> Pulling the thread back to you.

- **Your fragile top isn't only a cord problem** — a good part is acoustic. When a high note goes thin on a closed vowel, that's the F1 ceiling (section 6); the fix is F1 tracking + vowel migration (section 7): open the jaw, let the vowel drift more open, and the wall often dissolves.
- **The ring you want on your rock top** — the cut that lets a note carry without shoving it — is **twang** (section 9), not more force. That's a stamina win as much as a tone win.
- **Speech and singing pull opposite ways:** speech tunes formants for intelligibility, high loud singing tunes them for acoustic efficiency. That's why high notes are hard to understand — the formants have moved for resonance, away from the "correct" vowel. Don't cling to perfect diction at the top; let the vowel modify. Clinging to the pure vowel up high is fighting the physics.
