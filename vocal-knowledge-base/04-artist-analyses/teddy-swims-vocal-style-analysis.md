### Part 1: Understanding the Instrument – Teddy Swims' Vocal Anatomy and Influences

A singer's voice is a unique amalgam of physiological attributes and cultivated skills. For Teddy Swims, this combination has resulted in a remarkably versatile and expressive instrument.

#### 1.1. The Vocal Blueprint: Range, Tessitura, and Timbre

Teddy Swims is classified as a high baritone, a voice type that typically possesses warmth and richness in the lower and middle registers. However, what distinguishes him is an "extraordinary upper extension," reportedly reaching up to a C#6. This is a significant achievement for a male voice. The inherently thicker vocal folds in males generally make accessing such high pitches in a connected, powerful mixed voice—without sounding strained or "kettle-ish" —a considerable technical feat. While C#6 represents a peak in his absolute range, his functional tessitura, or the range where his voice is most comfortable and powerful, often lies in the upper baritone and tenor domain. It is in this area that his ability to project with clarity and emotional intensity is most consistently demonstrated.

His vocal timbre is consistently described as rich and soulful, possessing a characteristic warmth that is frequently enhanced by a controlled, gritty texture. This signature sound is not merely a product of his vocal fold vibration but is also actively shaped by the morphology of his vocal tract. Swims consciously modifies the shape of his mouth and throat to accentuate certain frequencies, thereby adding depth and richness to his tone. This active manipulation of resonators is akin to a musician tuning their instrument, demonstrating a sophisticated understanding of his own vocal production.

#### 1.2. Roots of the Soul: Musical and Developmental Influences

The distinctive quality of Teddy Swims' voice and style is deeply rooted in a confluence of early musical exposures and formative performance experiences. His father and a school teacher introduced him to soul music at an early age, immersing him in the sounds of luminaries such as Marvin Gaye, Stevie Wonder, and Al Green. These artists are celebrated for their profoundly emotive delivery, intricate phrasing, masterful use of melisma, and wide dynamic range—all prominent characteristics now evident in Swims' own vocal approach. This early immersion likely established a foundational understanding and appreciation for the expressive capabilities of the soul voice.

His passion for performance was ignited during his high school years through participation in musical theatre productions like *Joseph and the Amazing Technicolor Dreamcoat* and *Rent*, as well as Shakespearean plays. Swims himself has articulated the impact of this period, stating that, in his opinion, singing is "80% acting". This perspective underscores the importance he places on emotional connection and narrative delivery in his vocal performances, skills undoubtedly honed on the theatrical stage.

Interestingly, much of Swims' vocal technique development was self-directed. He has spoken about watching YouTube videos of singers to learn and refine his abilities. He specifically recalls analyzing the physical execution of vocal production by his idols, observing "What are they doing with their jaw? What is their face doing?" in live videos of artists like Marvin Gaye and Al Green. This demonstrates a meticulous, analytical, and proactive approach to his vocal education.

Further contributing to his vocal adaptability was his experience in a diverse array of Atlanta-area bands. His musical journey traversed alternative rock (WildHeart), post-hardcore (Eris), soul and hair metal cover bands, and even a role as the lead singer for the progressive rock/R&B/soul outfit Elefvnts. He was even a lead screamer in a metal band. This eclectic background is a cornerstone of his versatility and vocal resilience. Navigating such a wide spectrum of demanding genres—from the nuanced agility required in soul to the raw power needed for metal—would have necessitated the development of a robust and adaptable vocal technique. This extensive "cross-training" across genres likely provided a unique advantage. For instance, the breath control and resonance strategies required for metal screaming, if executed with an understanding of healthy production, can inform powerful belting techniques. Simultaneously, the subtleties of soul and R&B singing would cultivate agility, intricate phrasing, and emotional vulnerability. This fusion of experiences has likely contributed to a more multifaceted and resilient vocal instrument than if his development had been confined to a single genre. The deep immersion in classic soul, with its characteristic emphasis on melisma, dynamic variation, and a prominent mixed voice, likely shaped his technical priorities during his self-taught development, as these techniques would have been perceived as integral components of compelling vocal performance.

#### 2.1. The Seamless Bridge: Mastering the Mix Voice

The "mix voice" is a cornerstone of contemporary singing, referring to the skillful blending of the chest voice (lower, thicker vocal fold vibration) and head voice (higher, thinner vocal fold vibration) registers. The goal is to navigate the vocal range smoothly, eliminating noticeable breaks or shifts in tonal quality, particularly through the challenging *passaggio* (transition zone). Teddy Swims exhibits exceptional mastery of this technique. His performances, such as in "Lose Control," showcase effortless transitions from rich, resonant low tones to powerful, soaring high notes, reportedly reaching up to E4-C#6 in this specific song. This ability is central to achieving what is described as his "powerful yet emotionally charged sound".

A particularly advanced aspect of Swims' mix voice is his capacity to carry a "chesty" tonal quality—a sense of fullness and power typically associated with the lower register—into his higher notes. This is a common aspiration and challenge for many singers, as the voice can often thin out or become strained in the upper mix. Swims' proficiency here allows him to deliver dynamic vocal lines and sustain considerable power in higher passages without the sound becoming weak or overtly "heady," thus maintaining intensity and emotional impact throughout his range.

#### 2.2. The Soulful Grit: Vocal Fry, Rasp, and Controlled Distortion

A defining characteristic of Teddy Swims' vocal delivery is his use of grit, rasp, and vocal fry—textures that imbue his sound with a raw, soulful edge. Vocal fry is technically the lowest register of the voice, produced by very slow, lax vibrations of the vocal folds, creating a sound often described as "popping" or "crackling". Swims innovatively employs vocal fry, often at the incipience of phrases, not merely as a stylistic tic but as a tool to add texture, emotional weight, and an intimate depth to his delivery.

The more generalized raspy or gritty quality that permeates much of his singing is achieved through what vocal coaches have identified as "controlled compression and a touch of vocal fry". The term "controlled compression" is pivotal here. It suggests a deliberate and supported vocal coordination, likely involving specific adjustments in the larynx and pharynx (such as increased adduction of the vocal folds or a degree of false vocal fold engagement, managed with robust breath support), rather than simply forcing air through constricted vocal folds. This distinction is crucial, as it separates a skillfully produced, sustainable rasp from uncontrolled vocal noise that could lead to strain or injury. While these textures add significant character to his voice, it is acknowledged that their consistent or improper use carries a potential risk of vocal strain.

#### 2.3. The Art of Agility: Melisma and Vocal Runs

Melisma, the art of singing multiple notes on a single syllable of text, is a hallmark of soul, R&B, and gospel music—genres that heavily influence Teddy Swims. He demonstrates remarkable proficiency in executing "rapid melismatic runs," which demand exceptional "coordination, strength, and flexibility" from the vocal cords to navigate swift and precise pitch changes. His vocal agility has been noted as "quite agile for a male" , a significant commendation given that the typically thicker vocal folds in male singers can sometimes make achieving such dexterity more challenging.

These intricate vocal embellishments are not mere displays of virtuosity; they serve as powerful expressive tools, allowing Swims to elaborate on melodic ideas, intensify emotional peaks, and underscore the rhythmic nuances of a song. His capacity for agile runs enriches his performances, lending an authentic R&B and gospel feel and contributing significantly to the emotional impact of his delivery.

#### 2.4. The Power Source: Diaphragmatic Breath Support

Underpinning all of Teddy Swims' impressive vocal feats is a foundation of solid breath support, primarily achieved through diaphragmatic breathing. Swims himself has emphasized the critical role of this technique, stating in an interview, "What you need to do is breathe from here \[indicating the diaphragm\]... and then that's where you have all your power right there". He described this understanding as "the biggest thing that I learned" from a chorus teacher, further illustrating its foundational importance in his vocal approach. His explanation of the mechanics – the abdomen expanding like a "balloon should be filling up as you're breathing in and then when you're blowing out deflating" – is a classic and effective description of correct diaphragmatic engagement.

This method of breathing is paramount for sustaining long notes, controlling vocal dynamics effectively, and providing the necessary power and stability for demanding techniques such as belting, maintaining a strong mix voice in the upper register, and safely producing his signature rasp. While an observation was made regarding singers like Swims sometimes exhibiting shoulder movement during inhalation , it's important to differentiate this from detrimental shallow chest breathing, which Swims explicitly advises against. Advanced breath support techniques, such as *appoggio*, involve the expansion of the lower rib cage and engagement of intercostal and abdominal muscles, which can sometimes result in slight, almost imperceptible, movement in the shoulder girdle. However, the primary impetus for airflow and support remains deeply anchored in the diaphragm and core musculature.

#### 2.5. Painting with Sound: Dynamics and Phrasing

Teddy Swims' vocal performances are characterized by a sophisticated use of dynamics and phrasing, which transform his singing from mere note production into compelling musical storytelling. He adeptly varies vocal volume within phrases, creating crescendos and decrescendos that draw the listener in and amplify the emotional arc of a song. This dynamic control is intrinsically linked to his proficient breath management.

Furthermore, Swims employs strategic pauses and careful breath placement to manipulate the flow of the melody and influence the listener's emotional perception. This nuanced approach to phrasing demonstrates an intuitive understanding of how temporal elements in music can shape meaning and impact. Adding another layer to his rhythmic expression is the use of syncopation—the deliberate placement of vocal lines on offbeats or unexpected rhythmic accents. This technique not only enhances the inherent groove of the music, particularly in R&B and soul contexts, but also introduces elements of surprise and rhythmic tension that keep the listener engaged. These elements, taken together, reveal a singer who is not just focused on the notes themselves, but on how they are shaped, timed, and delivered to create a holistic and impactful musical experience.

Beyond the technical intricacies, the defining quality of Teddy Swims' artistry is his profound ability to convey emotion and forge a genuine connection with his audience. The interplay of these technical skills is crucial. For instance, a powerful mixed voice (Section 2.1) is essential for delivering emotionally charged high notes, but it is unsustainable without robust diaphragmatic breath support (Section 2.4). The controlled rasp and grit (Section 2.2) that add so much character and emotional weight rely on both that foundational breath support and fine laryngeal adjustments, which are often more accessible and safely managed within a well-developed mixed voice. Similarly, the agility required for melisma (Section 2.3) demands precise breath management and a relaxed, responsive vocal mechanism, all of which are hallmarks of good overall technique. Dynamic phrasing (Section 2.5) is, in essence, an application of sophisticated breath control and subtle resonance adjustments. Ultimately, all these technical components coalesce to serve the overarching goal of profound emotional expression (Section 2.6). This interconnectedness implies that aspiring singers cannot simply isolate one technique for mastery; rather, development in one area often supports and enhances capabilities in others, leading to a more holistic and versatile vocal instrument. This holistic development, demanding significant physical coordination and stamina, frames the vocalist in a manner akin to an athlete, dedicated to honing a highly specialized physical skill.

Furthermore, the approach to achieving more aggressive vocal textures, such as rasp or grit, as described by Swims and vocal coaches analyzing his style—emphasizing "controlled compression," "twang," and strong "support" —points towards a methodology that prioritizes vocal health. This contrasts with older notions where such sounds were often associated with vocal strain or damage. Modern vocal pedagogy, reflected in the understanding of Swims' technique, suggests that these textures can be produced sustainably if approached with correct physiological understanding and support, redirecting effort away from the throat and into the body's core support system and appropriate resonators. This is a vital consideration for any singer wishing to incorporate such sounds into their repertoire.

### Part 3: Emulating the Master – How to Sing Like Teddy Swims (Practical Guide)

Emulating a vocalist as distinctive and technically proficient as Teddy Swims is a significant undertaking that requires dedication, patience, and a focus on healthy vocal development. This section offers practical guidance, drawing from analyses of his techniques and general vocal pedagogy.

#### 3.1. Laying the Foundation: Essential Prerequisites

Before attempting the more advanced stylistic elements of Teddy Swims' singing, a solid technical foundation is paramount.

- **Vocal Health First:** The demanding nature of Swims' style, with its wide range, dynamic shifts, and textured sounds, necessitates an unwavering commitment to vocal health. This includes consistent vocal warm-ups before each practice session, maintaining adequate hydration to keep vocal folds lubricated, consciously avoiding vocal strain (stopping if pain or discomfort occurs), and listening attentively to the body's signals.

- **Mastering Breath Support:** As Swims himself emphasizes , diaphragmatic breathing is the absolute cornerstone of powerful, controlled, and healthy singing. This provides the engine for all other vocal techniques.

  - *Exercises:* Begin with foundational deep breathing exercises, focusing on the expansion of the abdomen upon inhalation and controlled exhalation. Exercises like sustained hissing (releasing air on an "sss" sound) can help develop control over the exhalation phase. The Farinelli breathing exercise, which involves a slow, deep inhalation followed by a very slow, controlled exhalation while maintaining an open ribcage, is also beneficial for building capacity and control.

- **Developing Ear Training and Pitch Accuracy:** Navigating the intricate melismas, subtle inflections, and broad range characteristic of Swims' style requires a well-developed musical ear and precise pitch control.

  - *Exercises:* Utilize pitch-matching applications or software to hone intonation. Practice interval training, singing various melodic intervals (thirds, fifths, octaves, etc.) accurately. Singing scales and arpeggios slowly and ensuring each note is in tune is also fundamental.

#### 3.2. Building the Techniques: Specific Exercises and Approaches

With foundational skills in place, singers can begin to explore techniques central to Teddy Swims' sound.

- **Developing Your Mix Voice:** Achieving a seamless blend between chest and head registers is crucial.

  - *Exercises:* Semi-occluded vocal tract (SOVT) exercises are highly effective. These include sirens or slides on an "NG" sound (as in the end of "sing"), lip trills (bubbling the lips), and tongue rolls (trilling an "R" sound). These exercises help to balance subglottal and supraglottal air pressure, facilitating smoother register transitions. Vowel modification exercises, such as sliding from an open vowel in chest voice to a more closed vowel in head voice (e.g., the "Hooty Exercise" from , which slides from a bright "AH" to a hooty "OO"), can also aid in navigating the break. The "MA" slide (as in "map") on a bright, slightly nasal quality is another exercise that can help beginners find a sense of mix. To address the challenge of carrying "chesty" resonance higher, as noted by some aspiring singers , incorporating a degree of controlled nasality or "twang" (a slight narrowing of the aryepiglottic sphincter, creating a brighter, more focused sound) can be beneficial, as it often helps to engage higher resonances without undue strain.

- **Achieving Controlled Rasp/Grit (Safely):** This is an advanced technique that requires caution and ideally, guidance.

  - *Exercises:* Begin by exploring vocal fry onsets—initiating sound in the lowest, "creaky door" register—to understand the sensation of lax vocal fold vibration. Gradually explore adding "controlled compression," which involves engaging breath support more firmly and potentially incorporating a slight laryngeal tilt or pharyngeal narrowing (related to "twang") to create the desired distortion without pressing from the throat. Some sources suggest practicing gentle "growls" with strong diaphragmatic support. One suggestion for achieving rasp involves "lots of twang and as much compression as is needed with the twang," always ensuring the effort comes from the body's support system, not the throat.

  - *Caution:* This technique should be approached gradually. If any pain, tickling, or discomfort is felt, stop immediately. Working with an experienced vocal coach is highly recommended for developing these sounds safely.

- **Improving Vocal Agility for Runs and Melisma:** Developing the flexibility and precision for Swims' intricate runs takes dedicated practice.

  - *Exercises:* Start by mapping out complex runs slowly, note by note, using a piano or a pitch app to ensure accuracy. Practice various scales: pentatonic scales at gradually increasing speeds are particularly useful for R&B/soul agility. Major and minor scales, as well as arpeggios across different vowel sounds, also build flexibility. Pay close attention to articulation, ensuring each note within a run is distinct and not slurred. Some observe that Swims often uses a relatively wide mouth posture during runs, which can influence resonance and clarity; singers can experiment with this.

- **Enhancing Dynamic Control and Expressive Phrasing:** Beyond notes and agility, shaping the musical line is key.

  - *Exercises:* Practice singing simple phrases or scales with smooth crescendos (gradually getting louder) and decrescendos (gradually getting softer) on a single, well-supported breath. Experiment with rhythmic variations, such as practicing syncopation by deliberately shifting accents to offbeats. To deepen the connection to phrasing, try the "lyric monologue" technique: speak the song's lyrics as if delivering an emotional monologue, paying attention to natural emphasis and pauses, then transfer these insights into the sung performance.

- **Cultivating Emotional Delivery and Authenticity:** Technical skill serves emotional expression.

  - *Exercises:* Actively engage with the lyrical content. Understand the story, the emotions being conveyed, and try to connect them to personal experiences or empathic understanding. The technique of "emotional memory recall" (accessing past personal emotions to inform performance) can be powerful but should be used with self-awareness and caution to avoid distress. Focus on how vocal color (timbre), dynamics, and articulation can be varied to reflect the emotional journey of the song.

#### 3.3. Practice Strategies and Mindset

The journey to developing a voice capable of Swims-esque feats is a marathon, not a sprint.

- **Consistency is Key:** Swims' own dedication, anecdotally involving "10,000 hours" of practice or "three years of two, three sessions a day" to hone his original music craft , underscores the necessity of consistent effort. Regular, focused practice sessions are generally more effective than infrequent, lengthy ones.

- **Listen Critically and Record Yourself:** Regularly record your practice sessions and performances. Analyze them critically, noting areas of strength and areas needing improvement. While comparing to Teddy Swims can be a benchmark, the primary focus should be on your own progress and vocal development.

- **Patience and Realistic Expectations:** Vocal development takes time. It's also important to recognize that every individual's vocal instrument is unique due to physiological differences. The goal should not be to become an exact replica of Teddy Swims, but rather to understand his techniques and incorporate elements of his style into your own authentic voice.

- **Study Teddy Swims' Performances:** Emulate Swims' own learning process by watching his live performances and interviews. Pay close attention not just to *what* he sings, but *how* he executes techniques, his physical engagement, his stage presence, and his emotional delivery.

- **Consider a Vocal Coach:** For guidance on advanced techniques like achieving a balanced mix voice or developing safe and controlled rasp, working with a qualified vocal coach is invaluable. A coach can provide personalized feedback and exercises tailored to your specific voice and developmental needs.

Teddy Swims' impact extends beyond his recorded work; his live performances are a crucial component of his artistry, showcasing his ability to connect with an audience on a visceral level.

Touring and delivering high-energy performances consistently place significant demands on any vocalist, particularly one employing the range, power, and textural variety of Teddy Swims. Modern performance environments also offer technological aids. In contrast, live performance is the ultimate test of a singer's foundational technique, emotional immediacy, and vocal endurance. While Swims undoubtedly benefits from the sophisticated production techniques available in the studio, his ability to deliver compelling live performances underscores the strength of his core vocal skills. This points to the reality for many modern performers who skillfully blend innate talent and honed technique with technological enhancements to achieve optimal and consistent sound quality across diverse performance contexts.
