# The Four-Machine Course

**A practical vocal training programme**

*Built for Aaron — reverse-engineered from real vocal science. Train from this, reread this.*

---

## How this course works

> You already have the information. This is where it becomes a physical skill.

Your voice is not one machine — it is **four working systems stacked on top of each other**: breath feeds the cords, the cords feed the vocal tract, and your nervous system runs the whole thing. This course trains them one at a time, in the order they physically stack, so that in every session you know exactly which machine you are working and exactly what it should feel like when it lands.

**How to use it.** Each session is 15–20 minutes. Do a session 4–5 days a week. Don't race ahead — you are building coordination, and coordination is reps, not reading. If you spent two weeks on Week 2 alone, that would be the right call, because the transition blend is your actual gap.

### The golden rule for the whole course

If a note goes thin, rough, or tight — **stop and ask which machine.** Never repeat a failing note louder. Repeating it louder just rehearses the fault and teaches your voice that the wrong coordination is the one you want. That single habit will make you improve faster than anything else in here.

## The four machines — quick reference

*The whole framework on one screen. Everything else in this course hangs off these four.*

| Machine | What it is | What it controls |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Breath** (airflow) | The power supply. Steady air metered out by the ribs and belly. | Whether the cords get an even stream (free) or spiking pressure (strained). |
| **2. Source** (the cords) | The buzz-maker. Two muscles trade off: thickness (TA) vs stretch (CT). | Pitch and register — chest / head / mix — and closure: breathy vs clear vs pressed. |
| **3. Filter** (vocal tract) | The sculptor. Throat, mouth, tongue, lips shape the raw buzz. | Vowels and resonance. Tuning its peaks (formants) makes notes ring and stay stable up high. |
| **4. Conductor** (nervous system) | The coordinator. Fine motor control, and involuntary tension under pressure. | Whether the coordination you built holds together live, across a set, under nerves. |

**The reframe that runs everything:** when a note fails, it is failing in *one* of these machines — and the fix is completely different depending on which. Most singers pour all their effort into machine 2 (push the cords harder) when the real problem is machine 1 or 3. Learn to spot which one, and you stop practising blind.

## The daily warm-up — open every session with this

> Two minutes. Never skip it. It sets the calm, wide, flow-first state everything else needs.

**Straw phonation (SOVT).** Hum a comfortable note through a straw — or through tightly pursed lips — so all the sound funnels through one small opening. Glide gently up and down through your easy range. No words, just the buzzy hummed tone through the narrow channel. The small opening creates a gentle back-pressure that balances the cords for you, so you feel flow-driven vibration without forcing anything. This is the single most efficient warm-up there is — it exercises breath, cords and resonance at once, on the lightest possible setting.

**It should feel like:**

- Easy, buzzy, and a little tickly on the lips.
- The throat completely uninvolved — no squeeze, no effort.
- Like you are *releasing* a steady stream, not pushing one.

## Week 1 — Breath: build the steady power supply

> **Machine:** airflow. **Goal:** flow, not pressure.

You cannot train the cords cleanly if the air feeding them is spiking and collapsing. So we lay the floor first. This week is quiet and unglamorous — do it anyway. The single fault it kills is **the ribs caving in the instant you start a phrase**, which dumps your air early and leaves nothing to hold the end of the line.

### Session A — The steady stream

**Do this.** Take a relaxed low breath — let the belly and lower ribs widen outward; the breath *drops in*, you don't heave the shoulders up to suck it in. Sing one comfortable mid note (G3–C4) on an "ah" and hold it as steady and even as you can for as long as it stays clean. Medium-quiet, not loud. Do 6–8, resting fully between each.

**Target.** The note does not waver, swell, or fade — the same size from first instant to last. If it wobbles or pushes, you are spiking pressure. Back off and let it sit.

### Session B — Straw phonation, extended

**Do this.** Your warm-up straw glides, but longer and with attention on evenness. If you have a glass of water, phonate through the straw with the tip 2–5 cm under the surface and watch the bubbles: small, even bubbles mean even airflow. Blow for as long as the bubbles stay even — the moment they turn ragged or the throat takes over, the rep is done. Stop there.

**Progression that matters.** Measure **steady-bubble seconds**, not total seconds. Chasing maximum duration means squeezing out the last of the air with collapsing ribs — which is exactly the fault you are removing. Log the number each session and push it a little further only while the bubbles stay even.

**It should feel like — when Week 1 is working:**

- Singing feels like releasing a steady stream, not shoving one.
- The ribs stay gently wide around the sound, holding their width as the note plays.
- The throat stays open and uninvolved. If it feels tired or tight after a note, you were pressing — go quieter.

## Week 2 — Registration: the transition blend

> **Machine:** the source (the TA/CT balance). **Goal:** trade weight for stretch through the passaggio.

**This is your week.** This is the actual gap — the A4–C5 zone where high phrases go thin or crack when you keep too much chest weight. Everything in Week 1 was to make this trainable. Take your time here; over-invest in this before moving on.

**The mechanism, in one line:** two muscles govern pitch — one thickens the cords (weight, power, your comfortable low range), one stretches them thinner (the higher, lighter setting). Going higher means trading thickness for stretch. Your instinct at the transition is to keep the thickness and shove it up. **That is the crack.** The fix is trusting the lighter, stretched setting *earlier* — before the instinct grabs power.

### Session A — Find the light setup on its own

**Do this.** Sing a bright, slightly whiny, slightly nasal "nay" (like a mocking "nyah-nyah") up around C5–D5 — deliberately light. Under-sing it. You are proving the thin-cord, stretched setting exists and that you can find it on purpose. Whiny and thin is *correct* here; you are isolating the machine, not performing. 5–6 easy attempts.

### Session B — The descending "nay" (the core drill)

**Do this.** Bright "nay". Start on C5 and descend a five-note scale — C5, B4, A4, G4, F4 — one "nay" per note. Keep it light and the same size all the way down: resist blooming into fullness as you drop into comfortable territory. The bottom note should be as narrow as the top. Quiet — half the volume you think you need. Then move the whole pattern up a semitone (C#5, D5…) until the top "nay" sits around F5.

**Why it works.** Coming *down* through the transition lets you arrive in the light coordination instead of dragging chest weight up into it. You learn the handover from the easy direction first. Only once the descending version is reliable to F5, reverse it — ascend F4→C5 holding the lightness. That is harder, so it comes second.

**It should feel like — when Week 2 is working:**

- Narrow and forward — buzzing behind the top teeth, on the roof of the mouth. Not big in the throat or chest.
- Almost too easy — an "am I even singing?" feeling. That is the target, not a mistake.
- The pitch arrives clean and immediately — no rough patch before it settles. Weight off means nothing for the note to fight.
- If the throat tightens or the sound gets louder to reach the top, weight is creeping in — drop the volume, keep it whiny.

## Week 3 — Formants: make the light note ring

> **Machine:** the filter (formant tuning). **Goal:** turn the thin, under-sung upper note into a resonant one.

Week 2 gets the note light. This week makes it **carry**. A vowel is a pattern of resonance peaks — formants — created by the shape of your vocal tract. As you climb, your pitch rises toward those fixed peaks; shape the vowel well and a peak sits right on the note and it rings for free; shape it badly and the note collapses. Do not start this until Week 2's lightness is reliable — tuning a badly-set note just makes a badly-set note louder.

### Session A — Feel a vowel go from strained to easy

**Do this.** Take a slightly fragile high note (say A4 or B4). Sing it on a closed "ee" — notice it feels tight or wants to collapse (the "ee" has a low first formant and your pitch has climbed above it). Now, on the same note, slowly open the vowel — let "ee" drift toward "ih" then "eh", opening the jaw a little as you go. Listen for the moment the note suddenly opens up and rings, with no extra effort. That is a harmonic locking onto a formant — tuning happening in real time. Find the exact shape where it blooms, on a few different notes.

### Session B — Jaw-opening on the ascent

**Do this.** Run "nay" or "ah" up through the transition again, and this time consciously let the jaw drop open more as you climb. Opening the jaw raises the first formant, keeping it above your rising pitch, so the vowel stays supported instead of pinching shut. Jaw dropping open on high notes is not drama — it is chasing that formant upward to stay ahead of the pitch. Every high belter does it, knowingly or not.

**It should feel like — when Week 3 is working:**

- The note gains a ring or ping — a bright core that projects — without you pushing harder.
- Like the sound found a resonant channel and slotted into it.
- Effort stays low; volume and richness climb on their own. That "free amplification" feeling is the tube doing its job.

## Week 4 — Integration: real songs, stamina, nerves

> **Machine:** the conductor. **Goal:** combine all three on real music, and hold it under pressure and across a set.

Coordination that only works in isolated drills is not singing yet. Now you connect the machines and put them on actual lines.

### Session A — Slow-motion on a real phrase

**Do this.** Take a high phrase from a song you are working — one that sits up in your transition zone. Sing it slowly and quietly first, applying the stack deliberately: steady flow (Wk 1) → keep it light through the transition (Wk 2) → open the vowel so it rings (Wk 3). Isolate the specific words where you add weight or where a closed vowel fights you — those are your problem coordinates. Work them one at a time. Only once the phrase is clean slow-and-quiet, bring it to tempo, and last add any rock colour back — **narrow first, grit second, always in that order.**

### Session B — Stamina and the reflex

**Do this.** Run two high choruses back to back. Watch what fails first: going **thin** (registration slipping back to weight), **rough** (pressure creeping in), or **tight** (tension). Whatever drops out first is the machine that fades under fatigue — that is the one to give extra maintenance reps.

**On nerves:** if the throat tightens or the breath goes shallow and high when you imagine performing, that is reflexive tension. You can't willpower it away, but you can re-establish the calm setup — a deliberate low, calm breath before the phrase, throat as uninvolved as in your quiet Week 1 drills — and give the tension less to grab onto.

**It should feel like — when Week 4 is working:**

- The high phrase is repeatable — chorus two is as clean as chorus one.
- Upper notes stay light-then-coloured rather than thick-then-cracking.
- When nerves push the throat to tighten, you have a physical reset to return to instead of just hoping.

## The one question that runs everything

> **"Which machine?"** — the diagnostic that turns aimless practice into targeted fixing.

| When a note does this… | …it's this machine | …so train |
|---|---|---|
| Thin or cracking on the way up under pressure | **Source** — registration | Too much thickness, not enough stretch. **Week 2.** |
| Strained / collapses on a closed vowel high up | **Filter** — formant | Pitch overshot the vowel's peak. Modify the vowel. **Week 3.** |
| Rough, tight, effortful, no stamina | **Breath** — pressure not flow | Meter the air; keep the ribs wide. **Week 1.** |
| Pitch slides — scoops up into notes, or sags off phrase ends | **Breath** — support / stamina | Set the air before the phrase; hold rib width to the end. **Week 1.** |
| Fell apart live but was fine at home | **Conductor** — reflex tension | Calm breath, open throat, light setup as a reset. **Week 4.** |

## After the four weeks

You don't finish and move on — you **cycle**. Keep the straw work as a daily warm-up, keep the "nay" as your registration maintenance, and keep applying Weeks 3–4 to new songs. The machines don't need relearning; they need upkeep. And any time a note fails, you always have the one question that runs the whole thing — *which machine?* Bring the answer and the fix follows.
