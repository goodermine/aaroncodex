## The short version

Aaron has a recognisable bright, forward rock/blues voice with real pub-stage
energy, better dynamic choice and substantially more upper-register courage than
earlier in the year. He does not need more volume, grit or a darker sound.

The next gain is repeatable ownership of the note:

> **Land the note first. Keep the ribs wide. Then let the colour arrive.**

The newest and most specific finding is in the Captain Cook *Pressure Down*
take: the problem is not that Aaron cannot find the notes or that he breaks.
Notes generally arrive, hold briefly, then slide as the phrase runs out of
breath. Early phrases tend to scoop upward; later phrases tend to sag downward.
That is a stamina and airflow-management pattern, especially around the C#4
transition.

## Current voice profile

### Musical identity

- Bright, forward and twang-capable; naturally suited to rock, pub rock, blues,
  funk and speech-connected material.
- Strong commitment to hooks and character. Aaron's bite, swagger and grit are
  assets when they are added after the pitch is secure.
- Increasingly able to perform a complete song rather than protecting himself
  from the hardest section.
- A lighter upper setup is currently more reliable than trying to carry heavy
  chest weight upward.

### Working range — practical, not an anatomical ceiling

- **Comfortable working core:** approximately G3–A#4.
- **Transition zone:** approximately A4–C5; this is where excess chest weight,
  grit or volume can make the tone tight or thin.
- **Accessible lighter upper notes:** around F5 in suitable coordination.
- *Livin' on a Prayer* remains a useful milestone: Aaron has carried the full
  song through its demanding upper material, including the former limit area.

These are coaching observations from recorded work, not a medical assessment or
a fixed limit. A note being detected is not proof that it is comfortable,
repeatable or appropriate to push.

## What is working now

1. **Performance commitment.** Aaron is willing to sing the whole song and stay
   emotionally involved instead of retreating when the material gets demanding.
2. **Tone identity.** The forward rock/blues colour is recognisable and useful;
   it should be refined rather than replaced.
3. **Upper-register access.** Lighter coordination has opened material that
   previously shut down. Transitional cracks are coordination feedback, not a
   reason to force more weight.
4. **Dynamic choice.** Recent work shows more than one intensity level. He can
   create excitement through colour, timing and phrasing rather than only by
   getting louder.
5. **Held-note potential.** The latest live *Pressure Down* take was unusually
   steady through the held notes and phrases. That proves the organised version
   of the voice is available under real performance conditions.
6. **Listener connection.** The Redcliffe and Captain Cook performances produced
   spontaneous positive audience feedback. The voice is communicating, not just
   completing technical exercises.

## Current technical priorities

### 1. Phrase-ending breath sag — current primary target

In the 25 July Captain Cook *Pressure Down* take, **25 of 51 phrase endings**
were identified as sagging. Aaron confirmed the audible pattern: the note lands,
holds for about a second, then slides down. There were no reported cracks or
register flips in the genuine trouble spots.

The late-song examples cluster around approximately 2:44, 3:01 and 3:25. The
most useful working interpretation is that the breath supply and rib expansion
are not being maintained through the end of the line. The words are not the
cause; they are simply where the remaining air becomes audible.

**Cue:** "Keep the ribs wide and let the air meter out slowly."

### 2. Early phrase scoops — secondary target

Early trouble spots in the same take rise into the note rather than fall away.
This is the older pressure-led arrival pattern: energy or colour begins before
the pitch is fully claimed.

**Cue:** "Place the note first, then let the bite arrive."

### 3. The C#4 transition

Several Pressure Down trouble spots sit around D4/C#4, close to the current
estimated transition area. Treat this as a coordination zone, not a hard break
or a reason to push. Keep the note narrower and lighter, especially when tired.

### 4. Repeatability under fatigue

Aaron can produce a strong isolated phrase and a convincing early chorus. The
next step is keeping the same setup after several songs or repeated choruses.
When the voice starts to give out, reducing force and releasing air more slowly
is more useful than trying to overpower the line.

## What this is not

- It is **not** a need for more volume or more grit.
- It is **not** evidence that Aaron lacks range.
- It is **not** primarily a vowel or diphthong problem in *Pressure Down*; the
  tested vowel-migration theory was rejected.
- It is **not** a reason to coach "voice quality" from the Captain Cook take. Its
  lower raw voice-quality reading is dominated by the tavern PA/room capture.
- Auto-Tune can tidy pitch after the fact, but it cannot replace the breath
  stability that prevents a phrase ending from sagging. The Polish service now
  has the Tune backend operational, so use it as a finishing tool rather than a
  substitute for the coordination work.

## Primary practice plan

### One main drill: Rib Cage Stationary Drill

Use this before singing *Pressure Down*.

1. Stand comfortably and let the lower ribs expand gently as you inhale.
2. Release a quiet, even exhale without allowing the ribs to collapse at its
   start.
3. Keep the throat passive; the exercise is about metering the air, not pushing
   it.
4. Stop before the final air is squeezed out. Reset and repeat several clean
   times.

**Pass mark:** the ribs stay gently wide as the airflow begins.  
**Cue:** **"Wide ribs, slow release."**

Then transfer once to the single phrase around 3:01. Do not grind through the
whole song when the aim is to teach one clean ending.

### Supporting transfer

Use **Sibilant Hiss** after the rib drill when the airflow needs to be felt more
clearly, then sing one phrase. On straw phonation in water, measure **steady
bubble seconds**, not maximum duration; chasing the last possible second can
collapse the ribs and rehearse the exact fault we are trying to remove.

## Next-take targets

### Pressure Down

- Practise the 3:01 phrase alone first.
- Take one comfortable breath before the line and keep the ribs wide through
  the final word.
- Aim for roughly 70–80% effort; let the microphone hear the tone without
  shouting into the ending.
- On the first phrase, land the pitch cleanly before adding rock colour.
- Record a short focused pass, then stop and listen. The question is: **did the
  note stay organised until the final word?**

### High rock material

- Use a lighter, narrower upper setup before adding grit.
- Keep high entries at controlled intensity rather than trying to reproduce the
  thickness of a layered studio vocal.
- If the tone becomes tight or thin around A4–C5, reduce weight first; do not
  chase the note with extra force.

### Live-set strategy

- Take a little more breath before long lines as the set progresses.
- Preserve the first clean beat of the note, even when the performance energy is
  high.
- When fatigue appears, simplify the colour rather than increasing pressure.

## Coaching language to keep

Use one cue at a time:

- **"Wide ribs, slow release."** — phrase endings and breath stamina.
- **"Place the note first, then let the bite arrive."** — phrase openings.
- **"Still first, style second."** — held notes, bends and grit.
- **"Lighter and narrower."** — upper transition work.

## Measurement and evidence rules

- Compare like with like: same song section, key, recording conditions and
  separation method where possible.
- For live, tavern or phone recordings, prioritise capture-fair results and what
  is directly heard; room-sensitive voice-quality readings are not a coaching
  diagnosis.
- Pitch trackers can produce octave errors. The isolated contour and Aaron's ear
  take priority when a tracker loses the fundamental.
- A score is meaningful only with current canonical provenance. Do not combine
  old rubric versions, cached numbers or manually adjusted scores into a trend.
- No recording can diagnose anatomy, injury or a medical condition.
